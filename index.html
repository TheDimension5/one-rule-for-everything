Albert Einstein's unfinished puzzle

One Rule for Everything

Einstein spent the last thirty years of his life hunting for a single rule that explains why apples fall and why magnets stick. He never found it. This is the story of what he was looking for, and you get to play with every piece.

Einstein spent thirty years trying to write one set of equations that covers both gravity and electromagnetism. He never got there, but every attempt taught physics something. This page walks through each one, with simulations you can play with.

From about 1920 until his death in 1955, Einstein searched for a unified field theory: a single geometric framework containing both general relativity and Maxwell's equations. Three main attempts, three failures, and a problem that is still open today in a different form.

Einstein's unification program tried to derive Maxwell's equations from spacetime geometry alone. Here are his three main attempts, what each got right, why each failed, and how gauge theory and quantum gravity transformed the problem.

A guided tour of Einstein's geometric unification program, from Kaluza–Klein through teleparallelism to the non-symmetric metric, with the component-counting arguments, the no-go results, and the threads that survived into gauge theory, supergravity, and string theory.

Chapter 1

Two kinds of pull

Some things pull without touching. Drop an apple and the whole Earth pulls it down. That pull is called gravity.

Hold a magnet near a paper clip and the clip jumps. That pull is electromagnetism. Electricity and magnetism turned out to be two faces of the same thing, so scientists gave them one long name.

By 1920, Einstein had a rulebook for both. Gravity had his own rulebook, which he finished in 1915. Electromagnetism had a rulebook written by James Clerk Maxwell. Two pulls, two rulebooks. Einstein thought that was one rulebook too many.

Two kinds of pull

Gravity and electromagnetism both reach across empty space. Both get weaker in the same way: move twice as far away and the pull drops to one quarter. Scientists call that an inverse-square law. Isaac Newton found it for gravity in 1687, and Charles Coulomb found it for electric charges in 1785.

In 1865 James Clerk Maxwell showed that electricity, magnetism and light are all one thing, described by four equations. In 1915 Einstein replaced Newton's gravity with general relativity. So by 1920 there were exactly two known forces, each with its own beautiful theory.

Two forces that behave so alike, described by two unrelated theories, looked to Einstein like a coincidence nature would never allow. He decided the two theories had to be pieces of one bigger theory.

Two forces, two theories

Newton's law of gravity and Coulomb's law of electric force have the same shape: F = Gm₁m₂/r² and F = kq₁q₂/r². Both are inverse-square laws acting across empty space. The differences are just as striking. Gravity only attracts. Electric force attracts or repels. And electromagnetism is enormously stronger: between two protons, electric repulsion beats gravitational attraction by a factor of about 10³⁶.

Maxwell's equations (1865) tie electric and magnetic fields together and predict waves moving at the speed of light. Einstein's special relativity (1905) grew directly out of them. Then general relativity (1915) turned gravity into something completely different: not a force at all, but the curvature of spacetime.

That left an odd picture. One force was geometry. The other was a field living on top of geometry. Einstein was convinced the resemblance between the two inverse-square laws was a clue, and that Maxwell's field would eventually turn out to be geometry too.

Marble and wood

By 1920 fundamental physics had exactly two interactions, each with a complete classical field theory. Maxwell: ∂μFμν = μ₀Jν with F = dA. Einstein: Gμν = 8πG Tμν. Both have massless carriers and inverse-square static limits. Both are gauge theories in the broad sense, one under U(1) phase changes, the other under diffeomorphisms.

The two sit differently in the equations, though. The Einstein–Maxwell system (Reissner–Nordström, 1916–18) already combines them, but electromagnetism enters only as a source on the right-hand side through its stress-energy tensor. Einstein called the left side of his equations fine marble and the right side low-grade wood. The goal of unification was to turn the wood into marble: make Fμν part of the geometry rather than a source for it.

There is also a structural asymmetry his program had to overcome. Gravity couples universally to energy-momentum, a rank-2 source, and obeys the equivalence principle. Electromagnetism couples to a conserved rank-1 current, and different bodies carry different charge-to-mass ratios. A geometric unification has to explain why one interaction is universal and the other is not.

The asymmetry to be explained

The classical data in 1920: a spin-2 field (the metric) with universal coupling to Tμν, and a spin-1 field (a U(1) connection) coupled to a conserved current. Both massless, both long-range, both with action principles: Einstein–Hilbert and −¼FμνFμν. Coupling them is trivial. Deriving one from the other is the problem.

Hermann Weyl made the first serious geometric attempt in 1918. He enlarged Riemannian geometry with a scale (conformal) gauge freedom and identified the scale connection with Aμ. Einstein's objection was physical: if lengths depended on path history, atomic spectral lines would depend on where an atom had been, and they do not (the second clock effect). Weyl's idea survived once London (1927) and Weyl (1929) replaced scale by phase, eiλ instead of eλ. That is modern gauge theory, and it is the sense in which electromagnetism really is a connection, on an internal U(1) bundle rather than on spacetime.

Einstein's program, stated precisely: find a single geometric structure on a four-manifold (or higher) whose field equations, derived from one action, contain both Gμν = 8πG Tμν and Maxwell's equations with the electromagnetic field on the geometric side. The three attempts below differ in where they hide the six components of Fμν: in a fifth dimension, in torsion, or in the antisymmetric part of the metric.

One number to keep in view: the electric-to-gravitational force ratio between two protons is about 1.2 × 10³⁶. A geometric unification must produce that hierarchy from geometry with no free parameter, or explain why the parameter is there.

For grown-ups

In Einstein's day these were the only two known fundamental forces. Both reach across empty space, both weaken with the square of distance, and both are described by elegant field equations. To Einstein the resemblance was too strong to be a coincidence. He also hoped a unified theory would explain the strange behavior of atoms, which he never accepted as fundamentally random.

Chapter 2

Gravity is a dent

Einstein's big trick, 1915

Here is the idea that made Einstein famous. Gravity is not a rope pulling you. Space itself is like a stretchy sheet, and heavy things make a dent in it. A marble rolling past the dent curves toward the heavy thing. That curving is gravity.

The Sun makes a dent, and Earth rolls around the edge of it. One lap takes a year.

Gravity is a dent

General relativity, 1915

In 1915 Einstein said gravity is not a force pulling through space. Mass bends spacetime, and things move along the straightest possible path through the bent shape. A planet is not being pulled around the Sun; it is coasting through a curved region.

This idea made predictions Newton's could not. Starlight passing the Sun should bend by a specific tiny angle, and in 1919 Arthur Eddington measured it during an eclipse. Mercury's orbit drifts in a way Newton could not explain, and Einstein's equations gave the exact number. Clocks run slower near heavy objects, and GPS satellites correct for it every day.

The stretchy-sheet picture is a cartoon. The real thing bends time as well as space, and it is the bending of time that does most of the work of making things fall.

Gravity as geometry

General relativity, 1915

Einstein's starting point was the equivalence principle: inside a freely falling elevator you cannot tell whether gravity is present. If falling feels exactly like floating, gravity is not a force acting on you. It is the shape of the spacetime you are coasting through. Free objects follow geodesics, the straightest possible paths in a curved geometry.

The field equations connect shape to content: Gμν = (8πG/c⁴) Tμν. The left side is curvature, the right side is energy and momentum. John Wheeler's summary: matter tells spacetime how to curve, and spacetime tells matter how to move.

The tests are precise. Mercury's perihelion advances an extra 43 arcseconds per century. Light grazing the Sun bends by 1.75 arcseconds. Time runs slow in a gravitational field, which GPS must correct by about 38 microseconds a day. Gravitational waves, ripples in the geometry itself, were detected by LIGO in 2015, a century after the prediction.

The simulation shows curvature of space only. Ordinary falling comes mostly from the curvature of time, which no rubber sheet can draw.

General relativity in brief

1915

Spacetime is a four-dimensional Lorentzian manifold with metric gμν. The Levi-Civita connection is fixed by the metric, curvature is the Riemann tensor, and free particles satisfy the geodesic equation. The field equations follow from the Einstein–Hilbert action S = (1/16πG) ∫ R √−g d⁴x plus a matter action, giving Gμν = 8πG Tμν.

Counting matters for everything that follows. The symmetric metric has 10 independent components. Coordinate freedom removes 4, and the 4 constraint equations remove 4 more, leaving 2 physical degrees of freedom per point: the two polarizations of a gravitational wave, or of the graviton. Any unified theory has to add electromagnetism's 2 physical polarizations (Aμ's 4 components minus gauge) on top of these.

The Newtonian limit lives in the time-time component, g00 ≈ −(1 + 2Φ/c²). That is why the rubber-sheet picture, which draws spatial curvature, is a metaphor rather than a model: spatial curvature near the Sun is real but contributes only a correction to orbits, the famous 43 arcseconds.

GR as a gauge theory of spacetime

1915

GR is the unique local, Lorentz-invariant, interacting theory of a massless spin-2 field coupled to its own stress-energy: Weinberg's soft-graviton argument (1964–65) forces universal coupling, and the Deser bootstrap (1970) reconstructs the full nonlinear Einstein–Hilbert action from linear Fierz–Pauli theory. This is the first no-go for naive unification: you cannot modify the spin-2 sector at low energies without breaking consistency.

The Bianchi identity ∇μGμν = 0 makes four of the ten field equations identities, mirroring diffeomorphism invariance, and forces ∇μTμν = 0 on the source. Einstein prized this feature because equations of motion for matter follow from the field equations (Einstein–Infeld–Hoffmann, 1938). He hoped a unified theory would be overdetermined enough that particle behavior, perhaps even quantum discreteness, would follow the same way.

Two generalizations matter later. Einstein–Cartan theory (Cartan 1922, Sciama and Kibble 1961) allows an independent connection with torsion sourced by spin density. And the tetrad formulation eaμ, with 16 components and local Lorentz gauge freedom, is the object Einstein reached for in 1928.

Finally, the quantum boundary. Quantized GR is perturbatively non-renormalizable: divergent at one loop with matter ('t Hooft and Veltman 1974) and at two loops in pure gravity (Goroff and Sagnotti 1986). The counterterms are Riemann-cubed invariants that cannot be absorbed. Unification today means solving this, which is a different problem from the one Einstein posed.

For grown-ups

General relativity: mass and energy curve spacetime, and free objects follow the straightest possible paths through the curved geometry. The rubber-sheet picture shows only the curvature of space. The real theory also curves time, and that part does most of the work of making things fall.

Chapter 3

Magnets have invisible hair

Now look at the other pull. Around a magnet or an electric charge there is an invisible pattern, like hair combed in a certain direction. Scientists call it a field. A compass needle points along the hair.

Notice something. The hair sits on top of space. It does not dent the sheet the way the Sun does. The sheet stays flat, and the field lives on it like paint on paper.

Einstein wondered: why would nature use two totally different tricks?

Magnets have invisible hair

Faraday and Maxwell, 1830s to 1865

Michael Faraday imagined lines of force filling the space around a magnet, and the pattern iron filings make is exactly that picture. A field is a strength and a direction at every point in space. A compass simply points along it.

Electric and magnetic fields turn into each other when things move. Wave a magnet past a wire and you get a current; that is how every generator works. Maxwell realized that a wiggling field makes a ripple that travels at the speed of light, because light is that ripple.

Here is the contrast with gravity. The field sits on top of space. Where a heavy object dents the sheet, a charge just paints a pattern on a flat sheet. Einstein found it hard to believe nature used two such different tricks for two forces that otherwise look so alike.

The electromagnetic field

Faraday and Maxwell, 1830s to 1865

The electric field of a point charge is E = kq/r², pointing away from positive charges and toward negative ones. Field lines start on positive charge and end on negative charge, and the closer together they are, the stronger the field. Magnetic field lines have no ends at all; they always close on themselves.

Maxwell's four equations govern E and B together. A changing magnetic field makes an electric field (Faraday's law), a changing electric field makes a magnetic field (Maxwell's addition), and together they let a disturbance travel at c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s. Special relativity then showed that E and B are one object: what looks like a pure electric field to one observer looks partly magnetic to another.

The simulation draws electric field lines for point charges by following the field direction step by step, and the compass shows the direction of E at your pointer. This is what Einstein wanted to rebuild as geometry: a field with a direction and a strength at every point, defined on spacetime rather than being spacetime.

Fμν: six components on a flat stage

In covariant language the field is an antisymmetric tensor Fμν with six components (three electric, three magnetic), built from the 4-potential: Fμν = ∂μAν − ∂νAμ. Maxwell's equations are ∂μFμν = μ₀Jν plus the identity ∂[λFμν] = 0. Gauge invariance under A → A + ∂λ removes two of the four components of A, leaving two physical polarizations.

Everything here is defined on spacetime. The metric enters only to raise indices and to supply √−g in the action. The field's energy-momentum TμνEM does curve spacetime, and the Reissner–Nordström solution shows how a charged mass warps the geometry, but the field itself is not the geometry.

The simulation integrates the Coulomb field of point charges with a midpoint method, seeding lines around positive charges and terminating them near negative ones. Arrowheads show the direction of E.

A connection on the wrong bundle

Geometrically, A is a connection 1-form on a principal U(1) bundle over spacetime and F = dA is its curvature. The Bianchi identity dF = 0 is automatic; d⋆F = ⋆J is the dynamical equation. The Aharonov–Bohm effect (1959) confirms that the connection, not only its curvature, is physical modulo gauge. Charge quantization is tied to the compactness of U(1).

This is the precise sense in which electromagnetism is already geometry. Its curvature lives in the fiber, an internal circle attached to each point, while gravitational curvature lives in the tangent bundle of spacetime itself. Einstein's program, in this language, was to find a single bundle or a single connection carrying both curvatures. Kaluza–Klein does it by making the fiber an actual spacetime direction. The non-symmetric theory tries to pack F into the metric. Gauge theory later went the other way and treated gravity as the gauge theory of the Lorentz or Poincaré group (Utiyama 1956, Kibble 1961).

What the simulation omits: radiation, retardation, and the magnetic field. A static Coulomb field is the electric part of F for charges at rest, which is all that Einstein's static solutions were ever tested against.

For grown-ups

Maxwell's equations describe the electromagnetic field as a tensor living on spacetime. In general relativity, gravity is not a field on spacetime at all: it is the geometry of spacetime itself. Einstein wanted electromagnetism to be geometry too. This simulation shows the electric field of point charges; a bar magnet's field has the same shape.

Chapter 4

Einstein's wish

So here is the puzzle. Gravity is the shape of the sheet. Electricity is a pattern drawn on the sheet.

Einstein's wish: what if the pattern is secretly a shape too? Then there would be only one rule, the rule for shapes, and gravity and electricity would just be two kinds of dent.

Slide the wish all the way up and see how the picture would change.

Einstein's wish

Einstein wanted geometry to explain everything. Gravity already was geometry, so the job was to make electromagnetism geometry too. There was a counting problem. Describing the shape of spacetime at a point takes ten numbers. Describing the electric and magnetic field at a point takes six more. Where can geometry hide six extra numbers?

Each of his three big attempts is a different hiding place. Idea one: an extra dimension. Idea two: a twist in spacetime. Idea three: a lopsided ruler. The slider shows the goal: turn the arrows drawn on the sheet into a shape of the sheet.

He had one more hope riding on this. Einstein never believed the fuzzy, random rules of quantum mechanics were the final word. He thought a complete field theory would produce atoms and their strange behavior automatically, the way the equations of gravity produce planets.

Where do six numbers go?

The metric at a point is a symmetric 4×4 matrix, which has 10 independent entries. The electromagnetic field at a point is an antisymmetric 4×4 matrix, which has 6. Together they make 16, exactly the number of entries in a general 4×4 matrix. That coincidence drove Einstein's last attempt and lurks behind the second one. The first attempt takes a different route and adds a whole extra dimension.

Einstein described his gravity equation as marble on the left (geometry) and wood on the right (matter and fields, written in by hand). A unified field theory would be all marble: a single geometric object whose equations contain both gravity and Maxwell's equations, with nothing added by hand.

He also expected more than unification. He hoped particles would turn out to be knots in the field with no singularities, and that the equations would be so tightly constrained that quantum behavior would follow from them. None of those hopes was fulfilled, but they explain why he kept going for thirty years.

The program, stated carefully

Einstein's criteria, drawn from his papers and from Physics and Reality (1936): a single geometric object; field equations from one variational principle; general covariance; and a solution space containing both Gμν = 8πG Tμν and Maxwell's equations, with Fμν on the geometric side. He added the hope that regular, particle-like solutions would exist and that the overdetermination of the equations would yield quantum discreteness.

Component counting frames all three attempts. Symmetric metric: 10. Antisymmetric tensor: 6. General rank-2 tensor or tetrad: 16. Five-dimensional metric: 15 = 10 + 4 + 1, gravity plus a vector potential plus one scalar. The counts work out suspiciously well every time, which is why the failures had to come from dynamics, not bookkeeping.

Contrast the unification that later succeeded. Electroweak theory unified by symmetry, an SU(2)×U(1) gauge group inside quantum field theory, and gauge fields are connections on internal bundles. Einstein's wish was vindicated in form, since gauge fields really are geometry, but with an internal geometry he never considered and a quantum foundation he rejected.

Unification as a well-posed problem

Write the target as a theorem to be proved: there exists a geometric structure and an action S such that its Euler–Lagrange equations split into (a) Einstein's equations for a symmetric metric constructed from the structure, sourced by (b) the stress-energy of a 2-form F, also constructed from the structure, that satisfies Maxwell's equations, with (c) charged sources of F that feel the Lorentz force. Each of Einstein's attempts satisfied some of (a) to (c) in a linearized or special case. None satisfied all three at the full nonlinear level.

Condition (c) is the one that usually failed and the one most often forgotten. Because GR derives equations of motion from the field equations, a unified theory must derive the Lorentz force the same way. Einstein–Infeld–Hoffmann methods applied to the later theories showed that it did not appear.

Two later theorems put walls around the program. Coleman–Mandula (1967): in a relativistic QFT with a nontrivial S-matrix, spacetime and internal symmetries combine only as a direct product, except through graded symmetries (Haag, Łopuszański, Sohnius 1975). Weinberg–Witten (1980): a theory with a Lorentz-covariant conserved stress tensor cannot contain a massless spin-2 particle, which blocks gravity from being a composite of ordinary fields in the same spacetime and is evaded only by changing dimension, as in holography. Neither applied in Einstein's classical setting, but both shape what geometric unification can now mean.

The counting argument itself is a warning sign. 16 = 10 + 6 holds for any rank-2 tensor, which says nothing about whether the extra six propagate like a spin-1 field, couple to a conserved current, or produce the right force between charges. Dynamics, not dimension counting, decides.

For grown-ups

A unified field theory in Einstein's sense means one geometric object whose equations contain both his gravitational field equations and Maxwell's equations. Gravity uses the 10 components of a symmetric metric tensor. The electromagnetic field needs 6 more. Where do you hide six extra numbers inside geometry? Each of Einstein's attempts in the next three chapters is a different answer to that question.

Chapter 5 · Idea one

A hidden tiny direction

Kaluza and Klein, 1919 to 1926

A mathematician named Theodor Kaluza wrote to Einstein in 1919 with a wild idea. What if space has an extra direction we can't see?

Think of a garden hose. From far away it looks like a line. An ant on it knows better: the ant can also walk around the hose. Kaluza found that if you add one tiny rolled-up direction to Einstein's gravity, the electricity rulebook falls out for free. Oskar Klein later said the loop is so small that no one could ever notice it.

Einstein loved it, then worried about it, then came back to it again and again for twenty years.

A hidden tiny direction

Kaluza and Klein, 1919 to 1926

In 1919 a mathematician named Theodor Kaluza wrote to Einstein with a strange idea. Write Einstein's gravity in five dimensions instead of four, then suppose the fifth direction is too small to notice. The five-dimensional equations split neatly into ordinary gravity, Maxwell's electromagnetism, and one extra leftover field.

Oskar Klein added the key detail in 1926: the fifth direction is a tiny circle, about 10⁻³² meters around, at every point in space. A particle's electric charge is its momentum around that circle. Because only whole numbers of waves fit around a circle, charge comes in whole-number lumps, which is exactly what experiments find.

The catch: the same math said the lightest charged particle should be far heavier than an electron, by a factor of about 10²². Einstein kept coming back to the idea with different collaborators until 1941. String theory revived it in the 1980s and still uses curled-up dimensions today.

Kaluza and Klein's fifth dimension

1919 to 1926

Kaluza's 1919 trick: write general relativity in five dimensions. A symmetric 5×5 metric has 15 components. Split them as 10 + 4 + 1: the ordinary four-dimensional metric, a four-vector, and a scalar. With the extra assumption that nothing depends on the fifth coordinate, the five-dimensional Einstein equations become the four-dimensional Einstein equations, Maxwell's equations for that four-vector, and an equation for the scalar. Electromagnetism falls out of pure geometry.

Klein (1926) made the fifth dimension a circle of radius R. Quantum mechanics requires the wavefunction to repeat around the circle, so momentum in the fifth direction is quantized, p₅ = nħ/R. That momentum is electric charge, so charge is quantized, and R can be fixed from the electron's charge: about 10⁻³² m, near the Planck length.

The problems were serious. The same relation predicts the mass of a charged particle to be around the Planck mass, 10¹⁹ GeV, about 10²² times heavier than an electron. The extra scalar field (later called the dilaton or radion) has no known counterpart and cannot simply be set to zero without contradiction. And 'nothing depends on the fifth coordinate' was an assumption, not a result.

Einstein published on this line in 1927, with Walther Mayer in 1931–32, with Peter Bergmann in 1938, and with Bergmann and Valentine Bargmann in 1941. Extra dimensions came back in string theory, where six or seven curled-up dimensions are required for consistency.

Kaluza–Klein reduction

1919 to 1926

Write the five-dimensional metric as ds² = gμνdxμdxν + φ²(dy + κAμdxμ)² with y ∼ y + 2πR and the cylinder condition ∂y = 0. The five-dimensional Einstein–Hilbert action reduces to four-dimensional gravity, a Maxwell term −¼φ³FμνFμν, and a kinetic term for φ. With κ² = 16πG, the U(1) gauge transformation of Aμ is a y-dependent coordinate change, y → y + κλ(x). Gauge symmetry becomes an isometry.

Compactification gives the Kaluza–Klein tower: modes einy/R with four-dimensional mass n/R and charge proportional to n/R. Unit charge then fixes R ∼ 10⁻³² m, and the mass of the first charged mode is of order the Planck mass. That is the wrong electron by twenty-two orders of magnitude.

The scalar cannot be truncated. Setting φ = const in the field equations forces FμνFμν = 0, an inconsistency noted by Thiry and by Jordan around 1948. Einstein's 1938 paper with Bergmann relaxed the cylinder condition to allow periodic y-dependence, and the 1941 paper with Bargmann and Bergmann explored it further, without producing new physics.

The non-abelian generalization (DeWitt 1963, Kerner 1968) makes the gauge group the isometry group of a compact internal manifold, and Witten (1981) showed that the smallest internal dimension carrying SU(3)×SU(2)×U(1) is seven, matching eleven-dimensional supergravity. Chiral fermions turned out to be hard to obtain from smooth compactifications, one reason string theory rather than pure Kaluza–Klein became the modern carrier of the idea.

What survives of Kaluza–Klein

1919 to 1926

In Einstein frame the reduction gives S = ∫ d⁴x √−g [R − ½(∂σ)² − ¼ e−√3σ F²] in suitable units: gravity, a dilaton, and Maxwell with a dilaton-dependent coupling. This is a consistent truncation of the full theory only when the dilaton is kept. The classical charge-to-mass relation of the KK tower kills it as a theory of the electron, and no charged massless mode exists in the tower at all.

Charge quantization is topological: it follows from the U(1) isometry of the circle, and the mechanism generalizes to any compact G. Non-abelian KK on a coset G/H yields Yang–Mills for G but also a set of scalars, and the chirality obstruction (Witten 1981, 1983) means that on a smooth Riemannian internal manifold the four-dimensional fermions come in vector-like pairs. Orbifolds, fluxes, and branes are how string theory evades this.

What became permanent: gauge symmetry as internal geometry; the KK tower as the generic signature of extra dimensions (searched for at the LHC as excited graviton and gauge modes, none found up to several TeV); moduli such as the radion as the generic cost of compactification, hence the moduli-stabilization problem in string vacua; and eleven-dimensional supergravity (Cremmer, Julia, Scherk 1978) as the low-energy limit of M-theory. Einstein's own contributions to this line were mainly attempts to remove the cylinder condition and to interpret the fifth dimension projectively, with Mayer. They added no new physics, and he returned to four dimensions.

For grown-ups

Kaluza–Klein theory is general relativity in five dimensions, with the fifth dimension curled into a tiny circle. The 5D metric splits into the 4D metric (gravity), a vector (Maxwell's potential) and a scalar field. Momentum around the circle shows up as electric charge. The idea lives on inside string theory, which needs six or seven such hidden dimensions.

Chapter 6 · Idea two

Twisted space

Einstein, 1928 to 1931

Einstein's own next idea: the sheet can do more than dent. It can also twist, like wringing out a wet towel. Maybe the dent is gravity and the twist is electricity.

He worked on this for three years. Newspapers around the world printed headlines about it before he was done. Then he found the math did not give the right answers for real magnets, and he let it go.

Twisted space

Einstein, 1928 to 1931

Einstein's next idea came in 1928. Instead of describing the shape of space with the ten numbers of a metric, describe it with four little arrows at every point, like a compass rose with four hands. That takes sixteen numbers. Ten of them give you the old metric, and six are left over, exactly the number needed for electromagnetism.

In this geometry, space has no curvature at all. Instead it has twist, which mathematicians call torsion. Einstein hoped the twist was electromagnetism. The New York Times covered the work for weeks before it was even published, and the paper sold out.

Within three years he gave it up. The equations did not reproduce how electric charges push and pull, and the six extra numbers turned out to be just the freedom to rotate the arrows, which changes nothing physical. Today the same geometry is used as an equivalent way to write ordinary general relativity, with no electromagnetism in it.

Teleparallelism

Einstein, 1928 to 1931

Replace the metric with a tetrad: four vectors at each point, forming a local frame of reference. A tetrad has 4 × 4 = 16 components. The metric is built from it using 10 combinations, and the remaining 6 describe how the frame is rotated, a Lorentz rotation at each point. Einstein wanted those 6 to be the electromagnetic field.

Distant parallelism means you can compare vectors at different points directly, using the frames, and every such comparison agrees. That makes the curvature zero everywhere but leaves torsion: parallelograms of small displacements fail to close. Einstein's field equations were written in terms of the torsion.

Élie Cartan wrote to Einstein in 1929 pointing out that he had described this geometry in 1922. Their correspondence is a small classic of physics history. Wolfgang Pauli was blunter and told Einstein the theory had nothing to do with physics.

The failure was concrete. Maxwell's equations did not come out except as a rough approximation, and the field equations were either too restrictive to have interesting solutions or too loose to determine the field. In ordinary general relativity the six frame rotations are pure gauge, so there was never room for electromagnetism there. The same geometry, with the right action, is now called the teleparallel equivalent of general relativity.

Tetrads and the Weitzenböck connection

Einstein, 1928 to 1931

With a tetrad eaμ, the metric is gμν = ηabeaμebν. The Weitzenböck connection Γλμν = eaλ∂νeaμ is flat by construction, R = 0, but has torsion Tλμν = Γλνμ − Γλμν. Three independent quadratic torsion scalars exist, and one specific combination, the torsion scalar T, gives an action equal to Einstein–Hilbert up to a boundary term. That is TEGR, the teleparallel equivalent of GR.

Einstein deliberately chose a different combination, because TEGR has local Lorentz invariance and the six extra tetrad components are pure gauge. To make them physical he needed an action that breaks local Lorentz symmetry, and he then identified the torsion trace Tμ = Tλλμ, or an antisymmetric combination built from it, with the electromagnetic potential or field.

The 1929 and 1930 papers, some with Walther Mayer, worked out identities and compatibility conditions using Cartan's methods. The outcome, in Einstein's own assessment, was that the systems were either overdetermined, with no nontrivial solutions beyond vacuum, or gave Maxwell's equations only in first approximation with no coupling to sources. Lanczos and others noted that no Lorentz force emerged. Einstein moved on in 1931.

The modern echo is exact. f(T) gravity and other theories that break local Lorentz invariance in the teleparallel setting are studied today and are known to carry extra degrees of freedom whose number and health are still debated, which is the same problem in modern dress.

Torsion as electromagnetism: why it cannot work

Einstein, 1928 to 1931

Decompose the torsion into its irreducible parts under the Lorentz group: a vector Tμ (4 components), an axial vector Sμ (4), and a pure tensor part (16). Einstein's identification sought Aμ or Fμν inside these. The most general quadratic torsion Lagrangian has three coefficients; TEGR fixes them, and any other choice propagates modes beyond the graviton. Hayashi and Shirafuji (1979) analyzed the one-parameter family and found the extra modes; later Hamiltonian analyses of f(T) theories find additional degrees of freedom that are strongly coupled or ill-behaved around most backgrounds.

The structural obstruction is the same one that stops the non-symmetric theory: a healthy massless spin-1 field requires a U(1) gauge invariance and a conserved current to couple to. Nothing in the tetrad's Lorentz frame freedom supplies either. The six extra components are local Lorentz gauge in the consistent theory, and once that gauge is broken to make them physical you get a broken-gauge theory with the usual pathologies, not Maxwell.

The healthy things nearby: Einstein–Cartan–Sciama–Kibble theory, where torsion is non-propagating and sourced algebraically by spin density, with corrections to GR only at densities of order 10⁴⁷ g/cm³; supergravity, where torsion is bilinear in the gravitino; and the Poincaré gauge theory of Hehl and collaborators, in which both curvature and torsion are gauge field strengths. Torsion has a settled place in modern gravity. It is just not electromagnetism.

The 1929 episode is also a case study in press and physics: the theory was reported by the New York Times before publication, Einstein was besieged by reporters, and Pauli wrote that Einstein had gone over to the pure mathematicians. Einstein's retraction, in the 1931 papers with Mayer, was quiet and complete.

For grown-ups

This was teleparallelism, or "distant parallelism": a geometry with torsion but no curvature, built from tetrads with 16 components instead of the metric's 10. Einstein hoped the extra six would be electromagnetism. It failed to reproduce Maxwell's equations properly. Teleparallel gravity is still studied today as an equivalent way of writing general relativity.

Chapter 7 · Idea three

The lopsided ruler

Einstein, 1945 to 1955

His last idea, the one he worked on until the day he died. Imagine a ruler that gives a different answer going from A to B than going from B to A.

The part that is the same both ways is gravity. The lopsided part, Einstein hoped, is electricity. It was beautiful. But when he and his helpers worked it through, electric charges in this world did not push and pull each other the way real ones do.

The lopsided ruler

Einstein, 1945 to 1955

Einstein's final idea, from 1945 until his death in 1955, went straight at the counting. The metric is a 4×4 table of numbers that is symmetric: the entry in row 1, column 2 equals the entry in row 2, column 1. That is why it has only 10 independent numbers. Drop the symmetry and a 4×4 table has 16. The symmetric part, 10 numbers, is gravity. The lopsided part, 6 numbers, is exactly the size of the electromagnetic field.

The simulation shows the flavor: a ruler that counts differently going one way than the other. The part of the count that is the same both ways is gravity, and the difference is the part Einstein hoped was electricity.

He worked on it with a series of assistants, including Ernst Straus and Bruria Kaufman, and put the final version in the last edition of his book The Meaning of Relativity, which went to the printer weeks before he died. It never worked. The most damaging test, worked out in 1953, showed that charged particles in this theory do not push and pull on each other at all.

The non-symmetric metric

Einstein, 1945 to 1955

Write the fundamental field as gμν = sμν + aμν, a symmetric part plus an antisymmetric part. Ten plus six equals sixteen, the full content of a general 4×4 tensor. Einstein took sμν as the gravitational metric and hoped aμν would obey Maxwell's equations. In one version he made the metric complex Hermitian, so that aμν is imaginary.

The connection was also allowed to be non-symmetric, and Einstein invented a new symmetry principle, transposition invariance, to pick out field equations. Papers with Ernst Straus (1946) and Bruria Kaufman (1955) refined the equations, and Erwin Schrödinger in Dublin developed a closely related version with the connection as the only variable. In 1947 Schrödinger announced his to the press as a completed unified theory, which caused a brief public falling-out between the two men.

The theory failed on physics, not mathematics. Joseph Callaway showed in 1953 that charged particles, treated the way Einstein and Infeld treated masses in general relativity, feel no electromagnetic force from each other. The antisymmetric part did not behave like light or fields either. And, like all of Einstein's attempts, it said nothing about quantum mechanics.

The 'different distance each way' in the simulation is a picture rather than the literal math: the distance formula only ever uses the symmetric part. What the antisymmetric part actually does is more subtle, which is part of why the theory was so hard to interpret.

Einstein–Straus–Kaufman theory

1945 to 1955

Take a non-symmetric gμν and a non-symmetric affine connection Γλμν, with the compatibility condition ∂λgμν − gρνΓρμλ − gμρΓρλν = 0 (the index order is where transposition invariance enters), the constraint Γμ ≡ Γρ[μρ] = 0, and field equations R(μν) = 0 and ∂[λRμν] = 0. They follow from L = √−g gμνRμν(Γ) with the constraint imposed by a Lagrange multiplier. Einstein distinguished 'weak' and 'strong' forms of the equations and went back and forth between them.

Linearize around flat space and the antisymmetric part satisfies a Maxwell-like condition, which is what sustained the hope. Beyond linear order the identification collapses. Callaway (1953) used the Einstein–Infeld–Hoffmann method to derive equations of motion for singularities and found no Lorentz force. Papapetrou's static spherically symmetric solutions carry a Coulomb-like aμν that does not interact. Infeld reached similar conclusions in 1950.

Schrödinger's purely affine version (1947) took only Γ as fundamental and derived a metric from the Ricci tensor, which requires a cosmological constant. It suffers the same interpretive problems.

The final statement is Appendix II of the 1955 edition of The Meaning of Relativity, with Kaufman's paper 'A new form of the general relativistic field equations' (1955) as the last co-authored work. Einstein's own late assessment, in a 1954 letter to Michele Besso, was that physics might not be based on continuous fields at all, in which case nothing of his program, gravity included, would survive.

Non-symmetric gravity: the no-go results

1945 to 1955

The Hermitian version sets gμν = gνμ* with real symmetric and imaginary antisymmetric parts. The transposition invariance that replaces the missing symmetry is invariance under simultaneous exchange μ↔ν in g and of the index order in Γ. The theory is not a gauge theory of anything: aμν has no invariance of the form a → a + dλ, so it cannot describe a massless spin-1 field. Its antisymmetric sector behaves like a 2-form (Kalb–Ramond type) field rather than a vector.

Definitive negative results: Callaway (1953), no Lorentz force at the EIH level; the absence of any conserved current that aμν couples to; and, for Moffat's 1979 revival as Nonsymmetric Gravitational Theory, with aμν reinterpreted as an extra gravitational field rather than electromagnetism, the analysis of Damour, Deser and McCarthy (1993) showing that the antisymmetric sector propagates ill-behaved modes around generic backgrounds, curable only by mass terms and potentials that change the theory substantially.

The idea has an afterlife in a place Einstein could not have anticipated. The antisymmetric 2-form Bμν of the string NS–NS sector couples to the worldsheet through the combination gμν + Bμν, exactly a non-symmetric metric-like object. It appears in the Buscher T-duality rules and in the open-string effective metric of Seiberg and Witten (1999), where its antisymmetric part becomes a noncommutativity parameter. B is not electromagnetism, but a non-symmetric tensor of this kind is a natural object in string theory.

Verdict on the program: as a classical geometric unification of gravity with Maxwell's field, it is closed. The obstruction is not that the equations are hard but that the target was wrong on two counts. Electromagnetism is a quantum U(1) gauge field, and there are four interactions rather than two. What remains open is the successor problem, in Chapter 9.

For grown-ups

The non-symmetric field theory replaces the symmetric metric with a general 16-component tensor. Its symmetric part carries gravity, and Einstein identified the antisymmetric part with the electromagnetic field. Schrödinger pursued a related version. The theory produced no Lorentz force on charged particles and said nothing about quantum behavior, so it never matched experiment. The "direction-dependent distance" here is a picture, not the literal math: the line element only sees the symmetric part.

Chapter 8

What we know now

Here is the twist Einstein did not see coming. While he hunted, other scientists found two more pulls hiding inside atoms. The strong force glues the middle of an atom together. The weak force lets one kind of atom change into another. Einstein was trying to unite two pulls when there were really four.

In the 1970s, scientists found that three of the four pulls, all except gravity, fit into one rulebook called the Standard Model. Gravity still will not join. It is the last one standing, exactly like in Einstein's day, only now from the other side.

What we know now

While Einstein hunted, the list of forces grew. In 1933 Enrico Fermi described the weak force, which lets a neutron turn into a proton and powers the Sun's first step in making sunlight. In 1935 Hideki Yukawa proposed the strong force, which holds atomic nuclei together against the electric repulsion of their protons. Einstein was trying to unite two forces when there were really four.

Then, in the 1960s and 1970s, the other three were joined in a way Einstein had not imagined: not by geometry but by symmetry, inside quantum mechanics. The result is the Standard Model. It says electromagnetism and the weak force are two faces of one electroweak force that split apart as the universe cooled. The W and Z particles that carry the weak force were found in 1983 exactly as predicted, and the Higgs particle that gives them mass was found in 2012.

Gravity is still outside. When you try to write gravity in the language of quantum mechanics the calculations give infinite answers. The two main guesses for fixing this are string theory, which reuses Kaluza's extra dimensions, and loop quantum gravity, which treats space itself as made of tiny chunks. Neither has been tested by experiment.

Four forces, three united

The four fundamental interactions and their carriers: gravity (graviton, hypothetical), electromagnetism (photon), the weak force (W⁺, W⁻, Z⁰) and the strong force (eight gluons). The last three are described by the Standard Model, a quantum field theory with gauge group SU(3)×SU(2)×U(1). Its unification is by symmetry: the electromagnetic and weak interactions are one electroweak interaction above about 100 GeV, split in two below that by the Higgs field.

The structural idea comes from Hermann Weyl's 1918 attempt to geometrize electromagnetism, reinterpreted in 1929: the phase of a quantum wavefunction can be chosen freely at each point, and demanding that freedom forces the existence of a field, the photon. Yang and Mills generalized this in 1954. Einstein's instinct that electromagnetism should be geometry was right, but the geometry is an internal space attached to each point, not spacetime itself.

Gravity resists. Its coupling constant has units of inverse energy squared, so quantum corrections grow without bound at high energy and the theory is non-renormalizable. New physics must enter by the Planck energy, about 10¹⁹ GeV. The LHC reaches about 10⁴ GeV. The three Standard Model couplings, extrapolated upward, nearly meet near 10¹⁶ GeV, hinting at a grand unified theory, but the simplest such theory predicts proton decay at rates already ruled out.

Candidates for the full picture: string theory, in which all particles are vibrations of one object, gravity is automatic, and Kaluza's extra dimensions are required; and loop quantum gravity, which quantizes geometry directly. Neither has made a confirmed prediction.

The Standard Model and the gravity problem

The Standard Model is a renormalizable gauge theory with group SU(3)c×SU(2)L×U(1)Y, spontaneously broken to SU(3)c×U(1)em by the Higgs mechanism. Electroweak unification (Glashow 1961, Weinberg 1967, Salam 1968) was shown to be renormalizable by 't Hooft and Veltman in 1971, and asymptotic freedom (Gross, Wilczek, Politzer 1973) made QCD a quantitative theory of the strong force.

Two caveats on the word unification. Electroweak theory keeps two independent couplings g and g′, so the Weinberg angle is a free parameter; a true merger into one coupling requires embedding in a simple group, the grand unified program of Georgi and Glashow (SU(5), 1974) and SO(10). Minimal SU(5) is excluded by proton lifetime limits above 10³⁴ years, and coupling unification works quantitatively only with additional particles such as those of supersymmetry, for which no evidence has appeared.

Gravity as an effective field theory is fine at low energies: Donoghue (1994) computed the leading quantum correction to Newton's potential, a well-defined and utterly unobservable term. The trouble is the ultraviolet. Perturbative quantum gravity is non-renormalizable, so predictions at the Planck scale require a UV completion. String theory supplies one, with gravity emerging automatically and extra dimensions required, and AdS/CFT (Maldacena 1997) shows that gravity in some spacetimes is exactly equivalent to a gauge theory without gravity in fewer dimensions. Loop quantum gravity attempts a background-independent quantization directly. Asymptotic safety looks for a non-trivial UV fixed point.

Notice the shape of the modern picture: gauge fields are connections on internal bundles, gravity is the connection on the tangent bundle, and fiber-bundle geometry unifies the description. Einstein's geometric instinct was vindicated at the level of language. The physics that made it work, quantum field theory, was the part he refused.

Where the pieces landed

Descent lines from Einstein's program into current physics. Weyl's scale gauge → U(1) phase gauge → Yang–Mills → the Standard Model. Kaluza–Klein → non-abelian KK → eleven-dimensional supergravity → M-theory compactifications. Cartan's torsion → Einstein–Cartan → supergravity torsion and Poincaré gauge theory. The Hermitian metric → Moffat's NGT (unstable per Damour–Deser–McCarthy) and, in another guise, the g + B combination of string theory. Every attempt left a structure that survived, and none survived as a unification of gravity with electromagnetism.

The precise status of gravity: pure GR diverges at two loops (Goroff–Sagnotti 1986), GR plus matter at one loop ('t Hooft–Veltman 1974). N = 8 supergravity is finite through at least four loops with indications of a divergence at seven (Bern and collaborators); whether it is finite is open. String perturbation theory is UV-finite order by order, at the cost of a landscape of vacua with no known selection principle. The black hole entropy count of Strominger and Vafa (1996) and the replica-wormhole derivation of the Page curve (2019) are the strongest quantitative successes of any quantum gravity framework, and neither is an experimental test.

Experiment: no signal of quantum gravity, extra dimensions, supersymmetry, or proton decay. GR passes every test, including binary-pulsar timing, LIGO/Virgo waveforms, and the Event Horizon Telescope shadow. The cosmological constant problem, a 10¹²⁰ mismatch in naive estimates, is the sharpest quantitative embarrassment at the interface of gravity and quantum field theory. Proposed tabletop tests of gravitationally induced entanglement (Bose and collaborators, and Marletto and Vedral, 2017) would establish that the gravitational field is quantum, which is not yet an experimental fact.

The one thing the modern theories share with Einstein's is the goal of eliminating what he called wood: parameters and fields written in by hand. The Standard Model has 19 of them plus neutrino masses, and no current theory derives them.

The puzzle is still open

Nobody has solved it. Here is what a winning theory would need to do, and how Einstein's three ideas measured up.

What is actually unsolved

The unsolved problem is no longer Einstein's. His goal, a classical geometric theory containing gravity and electromagnetism, is closed: electromagnetism is quantum and there are four forces. The living problem is quantum gravity: a theory that reduces to general relativity at large scales and to the Standard Model at small ones, and that makes sense at the Planck scale where both break down.

Why it is hard. Quantum field theory assumes a fixed background spacetime on which fields fluctuate; general relativity says the spacetime is the dynamical field. Quantum gravity must describe fluctuations of the stage itself. The relevant scale, the Planck length of 1.6 × 10⁻³⁵ m or the Planck energy of 1.2 × 10¹⁹ GeV, is fifteen orders of magnitude beyond the LHC.

Where clues might come from: black holes, whose entropy S = kA/4ℓP² ties gravity, quantum mechanics and thermodynamics together in one formula; the Big Bang, where Planck-scale physics may have left traces in the cosmic microwave background; gravitational waves, which now test general relativity in its strongest regime; and proposed tabletop experiments that ask whether gravity can entangle two small masses. The scorecard below is for Einstein's three attempts.

The successor problem

State it precisely. Find a theory that (a) reduces to general relativity coupled to the Standard Model in the low-energy limit, (b) is unitary and consistent at and beyond the Planck scale, (c) is background independent or at least fully diffeomorphism invariant, and (d) predicts at least one measurable deviation. Perturbative non-renormalizability rules out the naive approach. Effective field theory handles everything below the Planck scale but says nothing about (b) or (d).

Constraints from theorems: Weinberg–Witten forbids an emergent graviton from a Lorentz-invariant QFT in the same spacetime, which holography evades by changing the dimension; Coleman–Mandula and its supersymmetric loophole shape how spacetime and internal symmetries can mix; and the Bekenstein–Hawking entropy, S = A/4 in Planck units, demands that any quantum gravity theory count black hole microstates correctly, which so far only string theory has done in special cases.

The candidates divide by what they keep. String theory keeps QFT methods and adds extended objects, gaining finiteness and holography but paying with extra dimensions, supersymmetry, and a landscape. Loop quantum gravity keeps background independence and quantizes geometry, but recovering smooth spacetime and matter coupling remains unfinished. Asymptotic safety keeps ordinary QFT and bets on a UV fixed point. None has an experimental signature in reach. The scorecard below is for Einstein's three attempts.

The open problem, stated to the bone

Required: a UV-complete, unitary, diffeomorphism-invariant quantum theory whose low-energy effective action is the Einstein–Hilbert term plus the Standard Model, with computable Planck-suppressed corrections and at least one falsifiable prediction. Known partial results: the effective field theory of gravity is predictive below MP (Donoghue); perturbative gravity fails at two loops; holography gives a non-perturbative definition of quantum gravity in asymptotically AdS spacetimes, and none yet in de Sitter, which is the universe we live in.

Obstructions and clues in one list. Weinberg–Witten. Coleman–Mandula and the HLS loophole. The information paradox and its partial resolution through replica wormholes and islands, which is a statement about semiclassical gravity rather than a full theory. The swampland program, which tries to turn consistency with quantum gravity into constraints on effective theories (weak gravity conjecture, distance conjecture, de Sitter conjecture), all conjectural. The cosmological constant. The absence of any observed Planck-scale signal.

Einstein's original problem, classical geometric unification of gravity and Maxwell's field, has a definite verdict: closed, by the combination of no-go results in each attempt and the discovery that the target theory was quantum and that the interactions numbered four. His deeper intuition, that the right description is geometric and that hand-inserted parameters signal an incomplete theory, is the working assumption of every current candidate. Why this page can't solve it either

The missing rule has to work for gravity and for the fuzzy world of atoms at the same time. When you write both into one equation today, the answers come out infinite. Fixing that needs a brand-new idea, and probably an experiment we do not know how to build yet. That is an honest "not yet," not a "never."

Quantum mechanics and general relativity are written in different languages. Quantum theory works with probabilities on a fixed stage of space and time. Relativity says the stage itself bends and moves. Put them together at very small distances, about 10⁻³⁵ meters, and the equations give infinite answers. No microscope or particle accelerator can see anything that small, so the best clues come from black holes, the Big Bang, and gravitational waves.

The missing theory has to be unitary, background independent, and finite at the Planck scale, and it has to reproduce both known theories in their own domains. Each candidate has a piece of that and each pays a price for it. No experiment yet tells them apart, and no page can substitute for one.

There is no shortage of proposals. What is missing is a computation from any one of them that yields a number an experiment can check and that the other proposals do not also predict. Until then, the honest status is a set of partially consistent frameworks and an empty column of confirmed results.

The open problem is not a missing derivation within a known framework. It is the absence of a framework in which a diffeomorphism-invariant metric and a unitary S-matrix are simultaneously well defined, with a computable low-energy limit and a testable prediction. Any page claiming otherwise deserves the skepticism that met the 1929 headlines.

The end, for now

Einstein died in 1955 with unfinished equations on his desk. He knew he might not finish. He kept going anyway, because he believed the universe was too beautiful to need two rulebooks.

Maybe the person who finds the one rule is reading this right now.
